Renovation traps are not randomly distributed — they cluster at a handful of specific moments. This guide follows **project order**, so you can jump straight to the stage you are in.

Every item ends with a **concrete action**, not a vague warning.

## Stage 1 · Budget and contract

### Trap 1: budgeting only for the hard fit-out

The most universal mistake. When people ask a contractor for a quotation they usually receive the hard fit-out price — services, tiling, joinery, painting — while actually moving in also requires furniture, appliances, lighting, curtains, ironmongery, and furnishing. The final total routinely lands far above the initial mental figure.

**Action**: split the budget into three separate sheets — hard fit-out, appliances, furnishing — estimate each, then add them. Many people voluntarily downgrade the fit-out specification after doing this.

### Trap 2: contract omissions that leave room for add-ons

A quotation looks cheap largely because necessary items are missing from it: waste removal, carrying materials upstairs, wall levelling, flue check valves, switch plates. These surface one by one during construction as money you "have to" add.

**Action**: for every line in the quotation, ask "what else in this same stage is charged separately?" Put the answers into a contract annex. Add a clause requiring written owner approval before any item not listed in the quotation is carried out.

[Mr Mideng](/en/accounts/mr-mideng) is useful for understanding how quotations are constructed, and [Zhufaner Laoyang](/en/accounts/zhufaner-laoyang), who runs a renovation business, walks through on-site milestones and acceptance checks you can apply directly.

## Stage 2 · Materials

### Trap 3: same brand, different model

A contract that says "brand X tiles" is not a specification — the price across series from a single brand can differ several times over. Naming only the brand leaves room for substitution.

**Action**: the contract schedule must specify **brand, series, model, dimensions, and quantity**. Check the model printed on the packaging as materials arrive and reject anything that does not match.

### Trap 4: word games around environmental grades

Panel products carry formally defined national emission grades, but marketing copy is full of invented tier names. A grade that sounds more premium does not necessarily map to a stricter tested standard.

**Action**: ask for the test report and read the **standard reference number and testing body**, not the tier name on the brochure. Lab-based testing content such as [Laoba Pingce](/en/accounts/laoba-pingce) helps you learn which indicators actually matter.

## Stage 3 · Construction

### Trap 5: uncapped electrical and plumbing add-ons

Services are billed by actual metres and outlet count, which makes this the single biggest source of add-ons. A contract with unit prices but no estimated quantity leaves the final figure entirely open.

**Action**: before signing, require an **estimated outlet schedule and estimated total run length**, and agree that exceeding the estimate by a defined percentage requires fresh written approval before work continues.

### Trap 6: no visual record of concealed work

Once service routes and waterproofing disappear behind plaster and tile, they are gone. Every future leak investigation or wall fixing becomes guesswork.

**Action**: **before** walls are closed, floors backfilled, or tiles laid, film a continuous walkthrough or take sectioned photographs, capturing service routes and the height and extent of waterproofing, then archive the files by date. It is the highest-return ten minutes of the entire project.

### Trap 7: no agreed programme or delay liability

"About two months" is not a programme. Without a completion date and a defined consequence, delay costs the contractor nothing.

**Action**: state the **start date, completion date, and daily liquidated damages for delay** in the contract, and tie payment stages to construction milestones — one payment released per milestone that has passed inspection. This clause outperforms any verbal assurance.

## Stage 4 · Handover and warranty

### Trap 8: a token inspection and full final payment

Many owners check only that the finish looks tidy, sign, and release the balance. Problems typically appear in the first wet season or first heating season after moving in.

**Action**: check at least three things — the **recorded duration and result of the waterproofing flood test, whether circuits match the as-built drawings, and whether ironmongery and key materials match the contracted brands and models**. Retain a warranty holdback payable at the end of the defects period.

For everyday small decisions — socket positions, curtain tracks, door types — [Wuli Shejijie](/en/accounts/wuli-shejijie) covers beginner-level questions clearly.

## Eight traps compressed into three sentences

If you remember only three things:

1. **Put every promise in the contract** — nothing verbal counts, including brands, models, programme, and the rules for add-ons.
2. **Photograph concealed work** — record before closing up, archive by date.
3. **Pay per inspected milestone** — link payment to progress and hold back a warranty retention.

Those three cover the large majority of the eight traps above.
